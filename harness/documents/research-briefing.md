# Research Briefing Guide

## Purpose and Audience

Produce a human-first research briefing that supports discussion and
further direction. Agents can use it to get their bearings, then follow
links to research reports and workflow records for deeper context.

The briefing is a coherent account of reviewed research, not an assignment
log, a collection of reports, or a feature specification. It must stand
alone for ordinary reading without reproducing all supporting material.

## Document Structure

Use the following top-level headings in this order, beneath a descriptive
document title. Keep the required sections concise. Omit optional sections
when they add nothing; do not create boilerplate to fill the template.

### At a glance

Required. State the research purpose and a small number of principal
takeaways. Lead with what was learned and why it matters, not an account of
how the research was performed. Represent important qualifications fairly.

### Context and scope

Required. Explain the problem, the questions investigated, and important
boundaries or exclusions. Include enough context for a reader unfamiliar
with the research assignments.

### Findings

Required. Organise reviewed evidence by topic or question, not by subagent
assignment. Use informative subheadings that convey the main points.
Explain what the evidence shows, why it matters here, and any qualification
needed to interpret it correctly.

Use short prose, bullets, or comparison tables as appropriate. Do not force
every finding into the same internal layout. Integrate related evidence
rather than concatenating reports, and make material disagreements visible.

### Implications and choices

Optional. Explain what findings could mean for the feature. Distinguish
inference from evidence, and identify alternatives and trade-offs where
useful. Do not present proposals as agreed requirements or invent choices
merely to fill the section.

### Open questions

Optional. Include consequential uncertainties or decisions where further
discussion or direction would help. Distinguish:

- **Research uncertainty:** what the evidence does not establish, its
  significance, and whether further investigation is likely to help.
- **Product choice:** what requires human direction, with relevant options
  and trade-offs. Evidence alone cannot settle a preference.

Explain material limitations here, or alongside the affected finding when
that makes them easier to understand. Do not omit limitations merely
because this section is optional.

### Further detail

Required. Provide descriptive links to the research reports used, relevant
synthesis review entries, and the workflow record for current status and
outstanding work. Resolve links relative to the briefing's actual storage
location; do not link to the reusable workflow definition as a substitute
for the work-item record.

This section supports deeper reading. It does not replace references
alongside substantive claims in the body.

## Editorial Standard

- Write in direct, neutral, professional Australian English.
- Use progressive disclosure: orient the reader first, explain important
  findings next, and link to detailed evidence and execution history.
- Include enough evidence and qualification to support understanding, not
  every observation gathered during research. Do not impose a fixed length.
- Keep paragraphs short and purposeful, with one main idea each and clear
  transitions between topics. Avoid walls of text and lists that merely
  fragment a continuous explanation.
- Make headings informative. A reader scanning them should gain a useful
  picture of the research rather than see only generic topic labels.
- Use tables for genuinely comparable information. Avoid paragraph-length
  cells and tables that make the material harder to read.
- Define unfamiliar terms once and use terminology consistently.
- Distinguish evidence, inference, proposals, and agreed decisions through
  clear wording. Preserve uncertainty and conflicting evidence.
- Place references alongside substantive factual claims. Link to reviewed
  research and, where needed, its supporting sources. Identify relevant
  sections, dates, or versions when they affect interpretation. Do not imply
  that underlying sources were inspected if only a report was reviewed.
- Use descriptive Markdown links rather than opaque citation numbers.
- Keep research-process narration, assignment status, and review history in
  the workflow record. Omit generic introductions, repeated conclusions,
  and unsupported claims of completeness.
- Surface useful opportunities for discussion without adding a generic
  closing list of questions or implying that the user must approve every
  finding.

For substantial briefings, stable identifiers such as `F1` for findings and
`Q1` for questions may help discussion. They are optional. If used, retain
identifiers across revisions, do not reuse retired identifiers, and do not
renumber unrelated items. A new document version remains the reference for
its exact wording.

## Completion Review

Before marking consolidation complete, check:

- Can a human understand the briefing without opening the workflow record?
- Do the opening and headings reveal what matters and where the reader
  could discuss, challenge, or redirect the work?
- Does the opening accurately represent the body and its qualifications?
- Are findings integrated rather than pasted together from assignments?
- Are substantive claims traceable to reviewed evidence, with no new
  unsupported claims introduced during composition?
- Are material disagreements and limitations visible?
- Are evidence, implications, product choices, and agreed requirements
  clearly distinguished?
- Are research uncertainties separated from choices needing human direction?
- Are the structure, terminology, references, and formatting consistent?
- Has unnecessary detail, repetition, and process narration been removed?
- Can an agent follow the links to the exact research and review context
  used, and find the workflow's current status?

Revise composition problems before completing consolidation. Return material
research gaps or contradictions to synthesis rather than concealing them
through editorial changes.
